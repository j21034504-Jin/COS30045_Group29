About this site

How we built it

Where the numbers come from, how we cleaned them, and what they can and cannot tell you.

The data

Every chart uses the Bureau of Infrastructure and Transport Research Economics (BITRE) hospitalised injury data for road crashes, September 2023 release, covering 1 January 2011 to 31 December 2021. BITRE draws it from the Australian Institute of Health and Welfare's National Hospital Morbidity Database.

  • National file: cases and bed days by month, age, sex, remoteness, road user and what they collided with.
  • State and territory file: cases and bed days by state.
  • First Nations file: cases and bed days by First Nations status and remoteness.

A hospitalisation here means a person admitted to hospital after a traffic crash who did not die within 30 days. Bed days are the total days spent in hospital.

How we prepared it

We cleaned the data in KNIME, one workflow for all three files, and exported 9 small CSV files for the charts.

  • Kept traffic crashes only (410,884 hospitalisations).
  • Excluded people who died, using BITRE's own measure, and recalculated bed days to match.
  • Left out the few cases with unknown remoteness, age or sex, only on the charts that show that attribute.
  • Never estimated suppressed values: we used only tables BITRE published in full.
  • Checked every exported total against the raw data.

The charts are drawn with D3.js version 7. The state map uses simplified state boundaries.

What the data cannot tell you

  • Counts, not rates. We did not divide by population, so bigger states and groups naturally have more cases. Compare change over time, not raw size.
  • Counting changes. Victoria (from 2012) and NSW (from 2017) stopped counting emergency-department-only stays as admissions, which lowered their numbers. Bed days were counted differently from 2015–16, so they jump in those years.
  • Three files, three totals. The State (403,293) and First Nations (395,288) files have lower totals than the National file (410,884), so we never add them together.
  • 2020 was unusual. COVID-19 restrictions cut travel, and April 2020 was the quietest month in 11 years.
  • Serious injuries only. People treated without being admitted, and people who died, are not included.
